# Example Breakdown

## Method

I used six specialized publications as the primary sample:

- Lenny's Newsletter
- The Pragmatic Engineer
- ByteByteGo
- Newcomer
- Not Boring
- The Diff

Why these?

- They are meaningfully niche, not mass-generalist.
- They have large and visible subscriber bases.
- Their posts expose structure clearly even when partially paywalled.
- They represent multiple niche formats: product, engineering, startup finance, and tech analysis.

## Success Snapshot

These numbers are not all measured the same way, so treat them as directional:

- Lenny's Newsletter reported `1,000,000` subscribers in March 2025.
- The Pragmatic Engineer reported `1,000,000` subscribers in April 2025.
- ByteByteGo reported `1,000,000` subscribers in August 2024.
- Not Boring says it sends its newsletter to `250k+` subscribers every week.
- Newcomer says `100,000+` investors and leaders rely on it.
- The Diff is listed by LetterRanks as `65K` subscribers, marked public and estimate-based.

## 1. Lenny's Newsletter

Primary sources:

- [1,000,000](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/1000000)
- [How AI will impact product management](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-ai-will-impact-product-management)

### Structural pattern

Format: `reader-question article`

Observed structure:

1. Plain-English headline.
2. Clarifying subtitle.
3. Short issue-branding intro.
4. Direct reader question.
5. Personal setup and market context.
6. Poll, image, or evidence artifact early.
7. Strong thesis before deep explanation.
8. Quoted outside authority to reinforce the move.

### Why it works

- Feels personal without feeling casual.
- Reduces topic ambiguity immediately.
- The reader question creates built-in relevance.
- The writer's thesis shows up before the article becomes long.

### What to copy

- Start from a real reader problem.
- Put the answer's stance up front.
- Use one proof artifact early: poll, chart, quote, benchmark.

### What to avoid

- Long scene-setting before the central claim.
- Hiding the thesis until the middle.

## 2. The Pragmatic Engineer

Primary sources:

- [More than one million readers](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/one-million)
- [Becoming a Better Writer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/becoming-a-better-writer)

### Structural pattern

Format: `professional deep dive with issue map`

Observed structure:

1. Functional headline.
2. Subtitle that states why the topic matters professionally.
3. Reader question or practical prompt.
4. `In this issue we cover` list before the main body.
5. Sectioned explanation with `##` headers.
6. Concrete examples from recognizable companies or career situations.
7. Paywall only after orientation and useful value.

### Why it works

- It is extremely skimmable.
- It treats the post like a mini-course, not a stream of thoughts.
- It converts because the reader sees the whole outline before paying.

### What to copy

- Add a course-like table of contents.
- Make each section do one job.
- Use workplace examples the audience instantly recognizes.

### What to avoid

- Soft introductions without scope.
- Dense walls of prose without preview headings.

## 3. ByteByteGo

Primary sources:

- [1,000,000 Special](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/1000000-special-30-off-annual-premium)
- [The Must-Know Fundamentals of Distributed Systems](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/the-must-know-fundamentals-of-distributed)

### Structural pattern

Format: `high-clarity technical explainer`

Observed structure:

1. Topic headline with strong search intent.
2. Immediate explanation of why the concept matters in the real world.
3. One core problem statement.
4. Short bullet list or question list to define the challenge.
5. Explicit statement of what the article will cover.
6. First section heading arrives quickly.
7. Paywall after the reader already understands the frame.

### Why it works

- The article is optimized for utility and comprehension.
- It does not waste the first screen on personality.
- It establishes the technical problem before any heavy theory.

### What to copy

- Use the first paragraph to answer `why should I care now?`
- State the exact concepts covered.
- Use bullets to crystallize ambiguity or tradeoffs.

### What to avoid

- Starting with abstract definitions only.
- Long history lessons before the problem.

## 4. Newcomer

Primary sources:

- [About Newcomer](https://www.newcomer.co/about)
- [No Mere Reversion to the Mean](https://www.newcomer.co/p/no-mere-reversion-to-the-mean-data)

### Structural pattern

Format: `reported data-driven brief`

Observed structure:

1. Claim-forward headline.
2. Subtitle that tells you the data lens.
3. Immediate statement of what the market expected.
4. Fast reversal: `That’s not what happened.`
5. Numbers in the first few paragraphs.
6. Chart before the paywall.
7. One strong section header before lock.

### Why it works

- The article proves seriousness instantly.
- The reader gets a non-obvious takeaway before paying.
- The post feels like market intelligence, not commentary.

### What to copy

- Put one surprising stat above the fold.
- Use contrast language early: expected vs observed.
- Show one chart before the paywall if possible.

### What to avoid

- Leading with general industry vibes.
- Saving all numbers for later.

## 5. Not Boring

Primary sources:

- [About Not Boring](https://www.notboring.co/about)
- [A Market For (Almost) Everything](https://www.notboring.co/p/a-market-for-almost-everything)

### Structural pattern

Format: `narrative thesis essay`

Observed structure:

1. Headline names the broad idea.
2. Subtitle gives a playful list of specific entry points.
3. Subscriber CTA and sponsor block are separated from editorial.
4. Conversational opening.
5. Concrete anecdote before abstraction.
6. Thesis sentence emerges from the anecdote.
7. Repeated pattern examples widen the idea.
8. Clean synthesis at the end.

### Why it works

- The reader gets story first, theory second.
- The prose feels human without losing conceptual clarity.
- The essay turns one small moment into a generalized framework.

### What to copy

- Use one vivid real-world anecdote as the launchpad.
- Name the governing principle in one memorable line.
- Expand from one example to a wider pattern.

### What to avoid

- Abstract theorizing before the reader sees something tangible.
- Overlong sponsor sections mixed into the article body.

## 6. The Diff

Primary sources:

- [About The Diff](https://www.thediff.co/about/)
- [Your Life is (Almost) a Call Option](https://www.thediff.co/archive/your-life-is-almost-a-call-option/)

### Structural pattern

Format: `issue-based publication with one anchor essay`

Observed structure:

1. Headline for the main essay.
2. Secondary line advertising the rest of the issue.
3. `Contents` label.
4. House updates or network notes.
5. Anchor essay starts with a conceptual claim.
6. Early analogy.
7. Progression from concept to worked example.
8. Close with share/signup/member CTA.

### Why it works

- It turns a newsletter into a habitual issue product.
- It gives readers both variety and a flagship argument.
- The issue framing makes the publication feel bigger than a single post.

### What to copy

- Use this only if you truly have recurring columns or side items.
- Keep one main idea as the anchor.
- Use a contents block if the post does more than one thing.

### What to avoid

- Pretending to have an issue format when you really only have one essay.
- Letting side items crowd out the anchor idea.
